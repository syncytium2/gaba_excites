How each model was checked

Every model here is a port: its equations rewritten in TypeScript from a published source. Each was checked against that source, by running the original code where it exists and against the paper's own numbers where it does not. The checks are tests in the repository's suite, which runs on every commit, so a change that broke one would fail before it reached this site. Details are in each model's section and in section 7.

1. The pyramidal cell

The cell is the regular-spiking (RS) pyramidal neuron of Pospischil et al. (2008), the most common electrophysiological class of excitatory neuron in neocortex. It has a single isopotential compartment with three voltage-dependent currents: a transient sodium current and a delayed-rectifier potassium current, both in the Traub–Miles formulation, which make the action potential; and a slow, non-inactivating M-type potassium current, which produces spike-frequency adaptation. There is no calcium current and no calcium-activated potassium current.

The kinetics and parameters are transcribed from the model's ModelDB entry (accession 123623): the mechanisms HH_traub.mod and IM_cortex.mod, and the cell template sPY_template. Temperature is 36 °C, where both temperature factors in those files equal 1.

As published, this cell has Cm = 289.5 pF and a resting potential of −70.57 mV. Its input resistance at rest is 32.1 MΩ; the template's comment gives 34 MΩ, which is the leak alone, and the channels open at rest bring it down to 32.1. That is low for a pyramidal cell recorded in a slice, where 100–300 MΩ is common, and it is low on purpose: the RS model was fit partly to recordings in vivo, where background synaptic activity holds the membrane in a high-conductance state. The pyramidal cell starts from these published values, with the leak at the template's 28.95 nS (0.1 mS/cm²); the leak and Cm controls move away from them.

1a. The fast-spiking interneuron

From the same paper and the same ModelDB entry as the pyramidal cell: sIN_template. It uses the same two mechanisms, Traub INa and IKd, with IKd at twice the density (10 against 5 mS/cm²) and no M current, in a 67 µm sphere (Cm = 141.0 pF) with a leak of 0.15 mS/cm² (21.15 nS). It rests at −70.0 mV with Rin = 47.3 MΩ; the template's comment gives 48.

Those two differences are the lesson. With no slow current to build up, it does not adapt: on an 800 ms step of 800 pA it fires at 101 Hz from start to finish, where the pyramidal cell starts at 43 Hz and falls to 10 Hz as its M current builds. The extra K⁺ repolarizes faster, so the spike is narrower (half-width 0.56 against 0.70 ms) at the same threshold. Rheobase is 385 pA, and the rate climbs steeply above it: 191 Hz at 1.6 nA, about 300 Hz at 3.2 nA. Like the pyramidal cell, it is checked spike for spike against NEURON (see Checked against NEURON).

1b. The LTS cell: when hyperpolarization excites

The third cell of the same ModelDB entry: the low-threshold-spike (LTS) pyramidal cell, sPYr_template with the parameters of demo_PY_LTS.hoc, originally Destexhe, Contreras & Steriade (2001). It is the pyramidal cell's 96 µm sphere and its Traub INa and IKd, a weaker M current (0.03 mS/cm²), a small leak (0.01 mS/cm², 2.90 nS) reversing at −85 mV, and Huguenard's T-type Ca²⁺ current (0.4 mS/cm²) with a submembrane calcium shell:

I_T = g m∞² h (V − E_Ca) m instantaneous; both gates at V + 2 mV m∞ = 1 / (1 + exp(−(V + 2 + 57)/6.2)) h∞ = 1 / (1 + exp((V + 2 + 81)/4)) τ_h = (30.8 + (211.4 + exp((V + 2 + 113.2)/5)) / (1 + exp((V + 2 + 84)/3.2))) / 3^((36 − 24)/10) E_Ca = (RT/2F) ln(Ca_o / Ca_i), Ca_o = 2 mM Nernst, from the live Ca_i dCa_i/dt = max(0, −I_Ca / (2F · depth)) + (0.24 µM − Ca_i) / 5 ms depth 1 µm, I_Ca per area

It rests at −84.0 mV with Rin = 446 MΩ. At that rest the T channels are de-inactivated, so depolarization sets off a calcium-driven burst: rheobase is 39 pA, and 100 pA fires a pair 25 ms apart. Held at −64 mV by 60 pA, the T channels sit inactivated. A 500 ms step to −80 mV removes the inactivation, and on release the T current fires the cell 88 ms later. A step to −70 mV is not enough, and from the cell's own rest even release from −95 mV fires nothing. Whether hyperpolarization excites depends on where rest is.

What can do the hyperpolarizing matters too. The rebound needs Vm near −78 mV or below for long enough. A train of GABA PSCs cannot take Vm past E_GABA, and against the holding current it falls short of it. With E_GABA at −80 mV, a 100 Hz train does not rebound the held cell at any size up to 40 nS per event. At −85 mV it takes 10 nS; at −90 mV, 5 nS; at −95 mV, near E_K, where a GABA_B response reverses, 3 nS (tools/experiments/lts_gaba_rebound.ts). Like the other two Pospischil cells it is checked spike for spike against NEURON, rebounds included (see Checked against NEURON).

How it was checked. Every value above is transcribed from the paper's Tables 1–3 and Equations 1–24. There is no public code for this model, so there is no oracle to run against, as NEURON was for the pyramidal cell. Instead the port is checked against numbers the paper reports. The paper says −6 pA of applied current holds the cell at −70 mV; the port holds it at −70.09 mV. The paper's Fig. 7F shows the model firing 0, 0, 0, 1, 4 and 6 spikes during 500 ms steps of 0, 6, 12, 18, 24 and 30 pA; the port fires exactly those. Both matched on the first run, with nothing tuned. src/core/gnrh.test.ts holds them.

One ambiguity: Table 2 gives a fourth constant, d, for each I_h time constant (7.6 and 54.1 ms), which the printed Equation 15 does not use. It is read here as an additive floor on τ. Dropping it changes neither spike count above and moves the trough of a −30 pA step by 0.4 mV.

As published, the cell rests at −67.5 mV with no applied current, and its input resistance there is 506 MΩ, from the paper's 1 nS leak, which is the leak default; −6 pA is the Ihold default. The leak and Cm controls work as for the pyramidal cell. With so few channels open at rest and a Rin near 0.5 GΩ, a few picoamps move this cell as much as hundreds of picoamps move the pyramidal cell. The step presets are sized accordingly, and follow the paper's own protocol: 500 ms steps of 0–30 pA in 2 pA increments. Integration uses the same scheme and the same dt as the pyramidal cell. The Markov sodium states are advanced by the exact matrix exponential at fixed V, and at dt = 0.01 ms every spike is within 0.13 ms of a run at dt = 0.0025 ms.

1d. The mouse pancreatic beta cell

The fifth model is not a neuron. A mouse pancreatic beta cell fires on its own once glucose is high enough, in bursts whose rhythm is set by calcium and by metabolism, and that electrical activity is what releases insulin. The model is Bertram, Satin, Zhang, Smolen & Sherman (2004), the one later papers call the Dual Oscillator Model. One compartment with Cm = 5.3 pF and no leak, four currents, two calcium compartments, and the metabolism behind the KATP channel:

Cm dV/dt = −(I_K + I_Ca + I_K(Ca) + I_K(ATP)) I_K = 2.7 nS · n (V + 75) n∞ = 1/(1 + exp(−(16 + V)/5)), τn = 20 ms I_Ca = 1.0 nS · m∞(V) (V − 25) m∞ = 1/(1 + exp(−(20 + V)/12)), instantaneous I_K(Ca) = ḡ Ca²/(0.5² + Ca²) (V + 75) ḡ = 0.6 nS (Table 1) I_K(ATP) = ḡ o∞(ADP, ATP) (V + 75) ḡ = 25 nS (Table 1); o∞ from Magnus & Keizer (1998) Calcium: dCa/dt = f_cyt (J_mem + J_er), J_mem = −(α I_Ca + k_PMCA Ca), J_er = p_leak (Ca_er − Ca) − k_SERCA Ca dCa_er/dt = −f_er (V_cyt/V_er) J_er Glycolysis: dG6P/dt = κ (R_GK − R_PFK), dFBP/dt = κ (R_PFK − R_GPDH/2), F6P = 0.3 G6P Mitochondria: dADP/dt = (ATP − ADP exp((r + γ)(1 − Ca/r₁))) / τa, τa = 5 min; γ rises with glycolytic flux Nucleotides: AMP + ADP + ATP = 3000 µM, AMP = ADP²/ATP

Two oscillators share the cell. Calcium: each burst lets Ca²⁺ in, which opens K(Ca) channels and, more slowly, raises ADP (calcium slows ATP production), which opens KATP channels; the cell falls silent, both recover, and it fires again. Glycolysis: the enzyme PFK is activated by its own product, so glycolysis can oscillate by itself, about every 5 min, and each pulse of it raises ATP and closes KATP. The paper's stand-in for glucose is RGK, the glucokinase rate. At RGK = 0.4 glycolysis is steady and the cell bursts regularly, every 32.4 s here (the paper says ≈ 30 s). At 0.2 glycolysis oscillates and paces the bursts into compound, accordion or slow glycolytic patterns, depending on the two K⁺ conductances. At 0.3 it can do either, depending on where it starts.

How it was checked. Equations 1–24 and Table 1 were transcribed from the paper, with the authors' errata. The one piece the paper does not print, the PFK rate law (Smolen 1995), was written from the authors' published code and is cited to it (Sources). The port reproduces what the paper reports: glycolysis alone oscillates at RGK 0.2 and not at 0.4; at 0.3 it is bistable, resting at F6P 63.7 µM, FBP 9.0 µM (the paper: ≈ 64, ≈ 9.1) from one start and oscillating every 5.2 min (∼5) from another; and the whole cell gives the regular, compound, accordion and glycolytic bursting and the subthreshold oscillations of Figs. 3 and 5–8, with ADP in and out of phase with calcium as in Fig. 9. The authors' equations, integrated separately by a stiff solver from the same start, give 345 spikes in 6 min and burst periods of 30.3–31.6 s; the port gives 340 spikes and 32.4 s. The tests are src/core/beta.test.ts, beta-bursts.test.ts, beta-glycolytic.test.ts and beta-app.test.ts.

In the app the beta cell lives on the oscilloscope: its rhythms run from 30 s to 5 min, longer than any step sweep, and rheobase is not defined for a cell that fires on its own. A Glucose & K⁺ row on the amplifier sets RGK and the two K⁺ conductances, and a pattern menu picks one of the paper's figures, with a time base and speed to watch it at. Changing either keeps the cell running from where it is, as a dish would when the bath changes: turn glucose down and back up and you may find glycolysis no longer oscillating, the experiment of the paper's Fig. 11. The cell starts from the authors' compound-bursting starting point rather than its steady state, which at low glucose is unstable. With no leak its input resistance is about 26 GΩ, so 0.9 pA (the rheobase of the glucose-free cell, RGK = 0) sets the siren's current scale, and the leak control can add the leak a real recording would have. EGABA for this cell is the app's generic −80 mV, a placeholder: GABA's action on beta cells is the human cell's lesson (Braun et al. 2010), next on the roadmap.

1e. The squid giant axon (Hodgkin–Huxley)

The sixth model is where the subject began: Hodgkin & Huxley's (1952) description of the action potential of the squid giant axon, with only a Na⁺ and a K⁺ current and a leak. It is space-clamped: one patch of membrane, with no propagation, the paper's "membrane action potential". A propagating spike needs a cable, which is on the roadmap. The patch is 10 000 µm², so 1 µF/cm² makes 100 pF and 100 pA is 1 µA/cm²:

Cm dV/dt = −(I_Na + I_K + I_L) + I_inj I_Na = 120 mS/cm² · m³h (V − 50) α_m = 0.1 (V + 40)/(1 − exp(−(V + 40)/10)) β_m = 4 exp(−(V + 65)/18) α_h = 0.07 exp(−(V + 65)/20) β_h = 1/(1 + exp(−(V + 35)/10)) I_K = 36 mS/cm² · n⁴ (V + 77) α_n = 0.01 (V + 55)/(1 − exp(−(V + 55)/10)) β_n = 0.125 exp(−(V + 65)/80) I_L = 0.3 mS/cm² · (V + 54.387) every rate × φ = 3^((T − 6.3)/10)

These are the paper's equations in the modern sign convention, rest at −65 mV and depolarization positive, as NEURON's built-in hh mechanism writes them. A Temperature control scales every rate by φ, with Q10 = 3; it opens at 6.3 °C, the temperature of the paper's calculations.

How it was checked, two ways (src/core/hh.test.ts). Against code: NEURON 9.0.2 running its own hh on the same patch (tools/neuron_oracle_hh.py), with NEURON's rate tables turned off (it otherwise interpolates the rates from a 200-point table) and its leak reversal set to the paper's −54.387 mV (NEURON rounds it to −54.3). On 21 current steps at 6.3 and 18.5 °C, from no spikes to 280, depolarization block and anode break included, the spike counts match exactly and every spike is within one time step (0.01 ms). Against the paper: its equations, constants and computed action potentials, below.

The squid axon against Hodgkin & Huxley (1952)

The paper writes V as the displacement from rest with depolarization negative; here Vm = −(V + 65). With that change, every rate function (Eqs. 12, 13, 20, 21, 23, 24, p. 519) and every constant in Table 3 (p. 520) is the port's, exactly: the tests evaluate the paper's printed forms beside the port's at voltages from −120 to +40 mV and require agreement to twelve decimal places, steady states and time constants both, at 6.3 and 18.5 °C.

The tests hold both.

Voltage clamp, the paper's own method, is checked against the paper's solution for a clamped step (Eqs. 8, 17 and 18): at a fixed voltage each gate relaxes exponentially from rest, so the K⁺ current ḡKn⁴(V − EK) and the Na⁺ current ḡNam³h(V − ENa) are known exactly. Run with the other channel blocked, the two presets that reproduce the paper's Figs. 3 and 6 measure them within 0.5% (K⁺, at the end of each step) and 1% (Na⁺, the peak).

What it shows. It rests at −65 mV, and its rheobase for an 800 ms step is 225 pA (2.25 µA/cm²). Up to 600 pA it fires once or twice as the step starts, then stops. At 700 pA it fires all the way through, at 59 Hz; there is no slow firing in between, unlike the cortical cells, whose rate climbs from near zero. A step of only −300 pA, which holds it 3.3 mV below rest, fires a spike on release: anode break excitation. Warmed to 18.5 °C it fires faster and its spikes are shorter. Presets cover each of these, and two voltage-clamp presets repeat the experiments of the paper's Figs. 3 and 6, the K⁺ and Na⁺ conductance families its equations were fitted to. EGABA for this cell defaults to rest, a placeholder: the squid giant axon has no GABA synapse.

2. Leak, Cm, Ihold, and the measured Rin

A model file specifies densities per unit area. An experimenter reads whole-cell quantities off the amplifier. The simulator takes the whole-cell quantities and maps them onto the model:

  • Cm sets the size of the cell. Area = Cm / (1 µF/cm²). The active channel densities are held fixed, so a larger cell has proportionally more of every channel. The leak is not scaled with it. On the pyramidal cell, with the leak held at 28.95 nS, rheobase is 569, 561 and 584 pA at Cm = 145, 290 and 579 pF, but 129 pA with the leak cut to 5 nS (Rin 149 MΩ): rheobase is set mostly by the leak, not by Cm. Cm changes the speed: τm, the latency to the first spike, and how fast the voltage follows a synaptic input.
  • gL is a linear leak conductance, in nS, and it is an input. It opens on each model's published leak and nothing but the leak control moves it: not Cm, and not a change to any channel. That is what a drug does on a rig. Block a channel that is open at rest and the input resistance rises, because nothing quietly compensates.
  • Rin is measured, never set: the inverse slope of the steady-state current–voltage relation at the resting potential, active conductances included. It is below 1/gL because channels open at rest (mainly the M current) conduct too. It is shown next to the leak control, and measured again from the hyperpolarizing steps (see Measurements). If the channels hold the cell depolarized so that there is no stable rest, the simulator says so and Rin is undefined.
  • EL stays at −70 mV. Changing the leak therefore changes the resting potential only slightly, through the shifting balance between the leak and the M current.
  • Ihold is a constant current injected throughout. Before every run the cell is settled at Ihold for 3 s, with no synaptic input, so the slow M current starts each sweep at its steady state. Every sweep starts from that same settled state, as though each were recorded after a long interval.
  • τm = Rin·Cm, with the measured Rin, is shown as a derived value. With a single compartment it is also the time constant of the voltage response to a small step.
  • Current, in pA or in pA/pF. A switch beside the mode switch shows every injected and recorded current either in pA or divided by Cm, as current density in pA/pF, which at 1 µF/cm² is the same number as µA/cm². Density takes the cell's size out: the pyramidal cell's 561 pA rheobase is 1.94 pA/pF, the squid axon's 225 pA is 2.25. It changes only what is shown. The app, the simulation and the CSV keep every current in pA, and each number converts by the Cm of the cell it came from, a pinned F–I curve by its own.

Rin at the holding potential is also reported. Depolarizing holding current recruits the M current and lowers the effective input resistance. That is physiology, not a discrepancy.

3. The electrode: Rs, bridge balance, pipette capacitance

I_cmd ──► pipette node P ──[ Rs ]──► cell V ──► membrane │ Cp to ground Cm dV/dt = (P − V)/Rs − I_membrane Cp dP/dt = I_cmd − (P − V)/Rs V_recorded = P − b · Rs · I_cmd (b = bridge balance, 0–1)

An ideal current source drives the pipette. In steady state all of the command current reaches the cell, so series resistance does not change the membrane potential. It changes what is recorded. The recorded voltage is taken at the pipette, so it carries an offset of I·Rs during a step (the bridge error), and the amplifier's bridge balance subtracts a set fraction of that offset. With pipette capacitance Cp > 0, Rs and Cp form a low-pass filter on the recorded voltage, and some of the current injected during fast voltage changes charges the pipette instead of the cell. Spikes in the record then look smaller and slower than the real ones. Cp stands for the capacitance left over after capacitance neutralization. At 0 pF, the default, Rs affects only the record's offset.

Every measurement below is taken on the true membrane potential, so electrode settings change what the traces show but not the F–I curve. To see how a bridge error would contaminate a threshold measurement, unbalance the bridge and compare the recorded trace with the true Vm (the “show true Vm” option).

4. PSCs

Both inputs are conductance-based: the current each event produces depends on the driving force (V − E). This is what makes the GABA reversal potential matter. A current-based input could not shunt, and could not change sign as the membrane crosses its reversal.

I_syn = g_glu(t)(V − E_glu) + g_GABA(t)(V − E_GABA) one event: g(t) = A · (exp(−t/τdecay) − exp(−t/τrise)), A chosen so the peak equals g_peak
  • Glutamate: AMPA-like, E_glu = 0 mV (fixed). Defaults 0.5 ms rise, 3 ms decay, 3 nS peak.
  • GABA: GABAA-like, E_GABA variable from −100 to 0 mV. Its default depends on the cell: −80 mV for the pyramidal cell, and −36.5 mV for the GnRH neuron, the value measured in adult mouse GnRH neurons with gramicidin perforated patch, which leaves intracellular chloride undisturbed (−36.5 ± 1.2 mV, n = 16; DeFazio et al. 2002). That is depolarized relative to rest, which is why GABA excites these cells. Kinetics default to 0.5 ms rise, 10 ms decay and 3 nS peak for the pyramidal cell. For the GnRH neuron they default to the measured PSC: 10 ms decay (9.9 ± 0.25 ms in adult females), 1 nS peak and an instantaneous rise (Jaime et al. 2026). These are the best available, not ground truth: the decay depends on chloride, and intracellular chloride in GnRH neurons has been inferred from E_GABA rather than measured.
  • Each input has a mean rate. Poisson timing draws exponentially distributed intervals. Regular timing gives clock-like events. At high rates a regular train approximates a tonic conductance.
  • Event times come from a seeded generator (mulberry32). The seed depends only on the barrage number, the input and the sweep index. So changing the leak, Cm, E_GABA or anything else about the cell replays exactly the same events, and any change in the response comes from the change you made. “New barrage” draws a new set.
  • A rise time of 0 gives an instantaneous rise and a single-exponential decay.

A mean rate of hundreds of hertz stands for many synapses converging on the cell: 1000 Hz is a thousand synapses each firing at 1 Hz, for example.

4b. Noise

There are two kinds of noise, kept separate because they teach different things.

  • The amplifier's filter and recording noise. The recorded signal at the pipette, after bridge balance, has white Gaussian noise and optional mains hum (50 or 60 Hz, random phase per sweep) added to it, and the sum goes through the amplifier's output filter: a 4-pole low-pass Bessel at 1, 2, 5 or 10 kHz, or bypassed. The settings follow an Axopatch 200B's front panel. The filter runs at every integration step (100 kHz), as an analog filter acts before the digitizer: the analog prototype's poles (normalized to −3 dB at 1 rad/s: −0.9952 ± 1.2571i, −1.3701 ± 0.4102i) are discretized by the bilinear transform with the cutoff prewarped, so −3 dB falls at exactly fc. A Bessel delays every frequency by nearly the same time, so a fast event is smoothed and delayed but does not ring; a step overshoots by under 1.5%. The RMS you set is the noise as it reads through the 10 kHz filter, the way an amplifier's noise is specified, so a lower setting shows less of it (about √(fc/10 kHz) as much) and bypass more. None of this reaches the cell or any measurement. The app opens at 10 kHz with 0.2 mV RMS, typical of a whole-cell current-clamp recording.
    What it does to a spike, on the pyramidal cell's first spike at 700 pA (true peak 46.7 mV): at 10 kHz the recorded peak is the true one within 0.1 mV, 0.03 ms late; at 5 kHz, 46.5 mV; at 2 kHz, 45.3 mV, 0.18 ms late; at 1 kHz, 39.2 mV, 0.42 ms late. These spikes are about 0.7 ms wide, so even 1 kHz costs less than one might expect.
  • Membrane noise is a fluctuating current the cell does feel. It is injected at the membrane rather than through the pipette, so it produces no bridge error. It follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: Gaussian, mean zero, SD σ, correlation time τ, advanced with the exact update x ← x·e−dt/τ + σ·√(1 − e−2dt/τ)·N(0,1). It is current-based on purpose, so it adds fluctuation without adding conductance and leaves Rin unchanged. The Poisson PSCs are the conductance-based kind. The default is off.

Both draw from seeded generators, like the PSCs. The seed depends only on the draw number and the sweep, so changing the cell replays the same noise. Measurements are still taken on Vm, so membrane noise, being real to the cell, changes them, and recording noise does not. Near rheobase, membrane noise turns “does this step fire?” into a matter of chance. Measuring that probability properly needs repeated trials of each step, which is on the roadmap.

5. Integration

The integration follows NEURON's default fixed-step scheme. Each step of dt = 0.01 ms solves the linear system for V (and the pipette node, when Cp > 0) by backward Euler, with all conductances held at their values from the start of the step. The gates are then advanced at the new voltage by the exact exponential update for fixed V (Rush–Larsen), which is how the hh2 mechanism itself advances them. Backward Euler is unconditionally stable, which matters because Rs·Cp can be much shorter than dt. Traces are stored at 40 kHz (every 0.025 ms), like a digitizer.

The step is 0.01 ms rather than NEURON's usual 0.025 ms because the comparison below showed that at 0.025 ms spike times drift by several milliseconds over a second of repetitive firing. At 0.01 ms they stay within 0.2 ms.

The simulation runs in a Web Worker, in the browser. A 13-sweep, 1-second family takes about 0.2 s.

6b. The oscilloscope

The step protocol settles the cell before every run and replays a fixed family of sweeps. The oscilloscope runs the same cell continuously instead, and every control acts on it as you move it: turn the holding current and watch it start to fire, or switch GABA on mid-burst.

  • The same model and the same integrator. The oscilloscope advances the cell with the very function the step protocol uses, at the same dt of 0.01 ms. It draws PSC events and membrane noise as it goes, from the same seeds in the same order. So as long as nothing is changed, a scope run reproduces a step-protocol sweep sample for sample, and the test suite holds it to that on both cells.
  • Nothing resets the cell. A new leak, Cm or holding current takes effect from the present state, so you see the cell relax to its new behavior, slow currents included: the M current's adaptation, the GnRH cell's slow inward current (τ 1.5 s) and its h-current. Only switching models starts again, from a settled state.
  • Stimulus. The holding current is the DC level. A repeating pulse (amplitude, width, period) can be added on top. Its starting amplitude belongs to each cell: 700 pA for the pyramidal cell, above its 561 pA rheobase, and 24 pA on −6 pA for the GnRH cell, the paper's 4-spike step.
  • The screen writes the recorded voltage and the command current left to right at the chosen time base and wraps, with an erase bar ahead of the write head. Each pixel column keeps the minimum and maximum of every sample in it, so a spike reaches its full height at any time base. The amplifier's filter and recording noise are the step protocol's own, run at every integration step, so with nothing changed the scope records exactly what a step sweep records.
  • Trigger and Store. With pulses off, the screen free-runs. With pulses on, each sweep starts at a pulse onset and runs one screen, then waits for the next onset. Onsets during a sweep are ignored, as a scope's hold-off does. Every sweep therefore lines up with the stimulus. Store keeps the screen as a faint reference trace behind the live one (up to three, each labeled with its settings), so a change can be compared with the cell before it. A stored trace is shown only at the time base it was stored at.
  • Readouts are measured on true Vm, as everywhere else: firing rate over the last 2 s of simulated time (spikes counted with the same −20/−40 mV detector), mean Vm over the last 1 s, and Vm now.
  • Speed. The simulation keeps pace with the wall clock at 0.1×, 1×, 10× or 30×, and the time base runs from 10 ms to 1 min a division, for the beta cell's minutes-long rhythms. On an Apple M4 Pro the pyramidal cell runs 68–98× faster than real time and the GnRH cell 19–21×, with and without PSCs and noise, so 1× has a wide margin. The beta cell runs about 17× in Node on a cloud machine and 6.7× in a headless browser there, so 30× is a request a device may not meet. After a stall, such as a hidden tab, it resumes where it was rather than racing to catch up. If a device cannot keep up, the Speed readout shows the speed actually achieved instead of letting the trace stutter.
  • A scope run cannot be replayed. Rheobase and the F–I curve belong to the step protocol.

6c. The siren

These models will compute an answer for any current, however large. A small red siren blinks beside the mode switch while the cell is being driven past what a real cell would do; hover over it, focus it or tap it for the reasons. Every criterion comes from the cell itself, and they are the owner's choice of three:

  • Spike failure: a spike that no longer overshoots 0 mV, or depolarization block, Vm held above −30 mV for 50 ms or more. A normal spike spends about a millisecond above −30 mV.
  • Vm beyond the reversal potentials: Vm outside the span of the reversal potentials in play, those of the channels, the leak and any PSCs that are on. No conductance can take Vm there; only injected current can.
  • Current: injected current more than 10 times the published cell's rheobase (800 ms step) away from its published holding current. That is ±5.6 nA for the pyramidal cell, ±3.9 nA for the fast-spiking cell, ±390 pA for the LTS cell, and −6 ± 170 pA for the GnRH cell.

In the step protocol it reports which steps tripped it; in the oscilloscope it stays on while anything tripped in the last second of simulated time. The test suite runs every preset of every cell and requires the siren to stay silent, and requires each criterion to trip where it should. For example, the pyramidal cell at 12.8 nA fires at 400 Hz with overshooting spikes, past the current limit; both Pospischil cells are in depolarization block by 25.6 nA.

The controls sit in three panels directly below the recording window, in every mode, each folding to one line: the amplifier (clamp mode, holding current, Rs, bridge and pipette capacitance, the output filter, and on the oscilloscope the audio monitor), the acquisition software (step protocol, pulse train, or the voltage-clamp protocol and measurement windows), and the simulation: the cell, its leak and Cm, channel blocks, a model's own settings, PSCs, membrane and recording noise. The split keeps a property of the simulated cell from being read as an amplifier control: the cell's leak gL is not leak compensation, and the filter is on the amplifier, not among the noise sources.

6d. Old school, and the audio monitor

The audio monitor is in the oscilloscope, off until asked for. Listening to the cell was how a rig was run, and it still teaches: you hear the rate climb as you turn up the holding current, GABA change sides as E_GABA crosses its crossover, and depolarization block as the pops stopping while the hiss goes on.

  • Hiss and pop, the classic monitor: the recording noise as hiss (its loudness follows the noise RMS) and each spike as a pop. It is built from events, not by sending the trace to the speaker, so the worker's uneven delivery cannot make it crackle; sound is scheduled about 0.1 s ahead on the audio clock.
  • Tone (Vm), the later kind: a tone whose pitch follows true Vm, four octaves from −90 mV (200 Hz) to +40 mV (3.2 kHz), equal steps in voltage being equal musical intervals. Drift glides, PSPs blip, and spikes chirp; a bin that holds a spike takes its peak, so no spike is averaged away.
  • Sound plays at 1× while running, and is synthesized entirely in the browser, with nothing fetched.

Old school is a switch beside the mode buttons, on by default and in both modes, so switching between the step protocol and the oscilloscope does not change the look. Untick it for the light, slide-ready figures. It turns the page into an instrument panel: dark enamel, with the step view's charts redrawn in a palette stepped for the dark surface (sweeps run dim to bright with step amplitude), and, in the oscilloscope,

  • a green-phosphor tube with persistence (about half a second while the beam runs, held while paused, like a storage scope), a beam spot at the write head, and a dimmer trace where the beam moves fast, as on a real tube;
  • knurled knobs for every ranged control: drag (Shift for fine), scroll, or use the arrow, Page and Home/End keys; double-click or press Enter to type an exact value. Phones keep the ordinary fields;
  • analog meters for firing rate and mean Vm, whose needles have the ballistics of a moving-coil meter (damped, with slight overshoot). They read the same quantities as the digital readouts, smoothed further by the needle: an analog rate meter is a low-pass filter.

6e. Voltage clamp

The third mode holds the cell at a command and records the current, on the same cells, channels and integrator as current clamp. The amplifier holds the pipette at the command; the cell sits behind the series resistance Rs, so Cm dV/dt = (Vcmd − V)/Rs − Imembrane, solved by backward Euler with the conductances frozen over each 0.01 ms step, and the gates moved afterwards at the new V, exactly as in current clamp. Every step is stored (100 kHz). With Rs = 0 the clamp is ideal: V is the command, and each command step carries a one-step capacitive spike, Cm·ΔV/dt. Pipette capacitance is taken as compensated.

  • Nothing is drawn from the model's equations. No activation or inactivation curve appears until a protocol has run and you have said what to take, in a window of one segment: a Peak, with its sign chosen (Peak + the most positive current, Peak − the most negative), Max |I| (the value largest in magnitude, sign kept), the Mean, or a Cursor. Each is the plain operation it names: when the current is outward throughout a window, its Peak − is the smallest outward value, not a peak. Up to two measurements, each normalized (I/|I|max, or G/Gmax with G = I/(V − E_rev), where E_rev is the selected model's own reversal for the ion the protocol measures, taken from its channels: E_Na is +50 mV on the cortical cells and the squid axon, +54 mV on the GnRH cell) and fitted with a Boltzmann, or, for a tail family, read for where it reverses.
  • Measured on the recorded current, through the amplifier's filter and with its noise, as on a rig. (Current clamp measures true Vm; this is the owner's choice for voltage clamp.) The true current and the cell's true Vm can be shown behind the record, and the I–V plotted against true Vm as well as against the command.
  • Protocols are a holding potential, a list of segments, and one segment that varies across the family, in voltage or in duration: activation (I–V), steady-state inactivation, tail currents and recovery from inactivation are presets, and the segments can be edited.
  • Channels can be blocked, one by one, as a saturating dose of a selective blocker would; the block carries over to current clamp. Blocking K⁺ cleans up an inactivation curve, as TEA or Cs⁺ does on a rig.
  • The pyramidal cell is too big to clamp. Its 290 pF soma carries about 270 nA of sodium current at 0 mV. Behind even 2 MΩ the clamp loses it: at a −40 mV command the cell fires an unclamped action current past +30 mV, and the measured I–V is the classic escape. The mode therefore opens with an ideal clamp, Rs = 0, and the Rs dial shows the escape; shrinking Cm (channel densities stay fixed) brings the currents down, as a smaller cell would.
  • The filter distorts the fast sodium peak. The activation preset measures from 0.1 ms into each step, to catch the sodium peak that on the fast cells comes before 0.2 ms, and opens with the filter off. On the pyramidal cell its fitted G–V has V½ −14.3 mV and k −12.6 mV. Through the 10 kHz filter (the default elsewhere) it is −9.2 and −15.8 mV, and at 5 kHz −9.8 and −15.4 mV: the filter blunts the fastest peaks, and its ring after the one-step capacitive transient, −10 to −34 nA at 0.1 ms on the cortical cells, reaches into the window. Measured from 0.2 ms the same cell gave −20.5 and −8.5 mV unfiltered and −17.3 and −10.6 mV at 10 kHz.

7. How each model was checked, in detail

Voltage clamp was checked the same way, with NEURON's own clamp, SEClamp: an ideal source behind a series resistance, the circuit the port solves. Activation and inactivation families at 0.001 MΩ, and activation at 2 MΩ, where the cell escapes the clamp: every sample 0.2 ms apart agrees within 10 pA and 0.01 mV, and every peak at full resolution within 0.05%; the largest differences seen were 1 pA, and 3 pA on 58 nA at 2 MΩ (tools/neuron_oracle_vc.py, src/core/vclamp.test.ts). NEURON's 0.001 MΩ is not quite ideal: 262 nA of sodium current through it is a 0.26 mV error, which the port reproduces at the same resistance.

In current clamp, the port was run against NEURON 9.0.2 executing the original ModelDB mechanisms (repository commit 8dfc13f): the same cell, a 3 s settle, then an 800 ms step, with dt = 0.01 ms and implicit Euler in both. The LTS cell is checked against sPYr_template with demo_PY_LTS.hoc's parameters, with IT_huguenard.mod and cadecay_destexhe.mod compiled beside the other two. There are four families of 500 ms steps, each settled 3 s at its own holding current: depolarizing steps from rest (−84 mV), and hyperpolarizing steps held at 60, 50 and 30 pA (−64, −66 and −71 mV). Spike counts agree exactly on all 12 sweeps, bursts and rebounds alike, and every spike time agrees to within 0.010 ms. The calcium shell reads the calcium current from the start of each step, before the voltage solve, as NEURON's does.

The electrode and synapse models are not part of the published cell, so NEURON cannot check them. The test suite checks them separately. With Cp = 0, changing Rs leaves Vm identical to the last bit. An unbalanced bridge offsets the record by exactly I·Rs. A single PSC peaks at exactly its set conductance. GABA reversing at the resting potential leaves Vm unmoved. Poisson trains have the requested rate.

8. When GABA excites

The simulator is named for this result. The table shows rheobase (bisected to 1 pA; 800 ms step) on the published cell, with clock-like GABA at 200 Hz, 3 nS peak, 0.5/10 ms, as E_GABA moves:

Two points come out of this table. First, depolarizing is not the same as excitatory. Between rest and about −50 mV, GABA depolarizes the cell and still makes it harder to fire, because the open conductance shunts the injected current. Second, the crossover sits at about −50 mV, some 11 mV below the −39 mV spike threshold. What matters is where Vm sits while the step charges the membrane toward threshold. Below the crossover, GABA pulls Vm down toward E_GABA; above it, GABA pushes Vm up. With Poisson rather than clock-like timing the numbers move by a few tens of pA from barrage to barrage, but the pattern holds. src/core/gaba.test.ts locks these values in.

9. What it leaves out

  • One compartment. There are no dendrites, no axon initial segment and no space clamp problem. Inputs arrive at the soma. Real distal inputs are filtered by the dendrites, and shunting depends on where inhibition lands.
  • Three active currents. There is no h-current (so no sag), no calcium or calcium-activated potassium currents (so no bursting and no medium or slow afterhyperpolarization), and no persistent sodium current. Threshold and firing pattern are the RS model's, not those of any particular recorded cell.
  • Fixed reversal potentials. E_GABA is a parameter, not an outcome. Chloride and bicarbonate do not move with activity. Sustained GABAA activity in a real cell can shift E_GABA, and this simulator does not.
  • AMPA and GABAA only. There is no NMDA voltage dependence, no GABAB, no short-term plasticity, and no correlations between inputs.
  • Current clamp only, with an idealized current source and a single lumped pipette capacitance. The amplifier is idealized apart from its output filter and recording noise.
  • The published pyramidal cell is in a high-conductance state, with Rin = 32 MΩ, as published. Cutting the leak so that Rin reaches slice values (5 nS gives 149 MΩ) makes it far more excitable.

10. Running it without a browser

The same code runs from a command line, with Node 22.18 or later and no build step:

git clone https://github.com/syncytium2/gaba_excites && cd gaba_excites && npm install node scripts/fi.ts # published cell, −200…1000 pA node scripts/fi.ts --gleak 5 --cm 120 --steps 0,50,100,150,200 node scripts/fi.ts --gaba 200,3,0.5,10,-60 --regular --rheobase

It writes CSV to stdout, with the settings as # lines above the table.
